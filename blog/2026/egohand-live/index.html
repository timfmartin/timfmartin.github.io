Teleoperating robot hands with one camera: making NVIDIA EgoHand run in real time

Created on November 25, 2026

2026   ·   robotics   teleoperation   isaac-sim   ·   projects

egohand-live lets me teleoperate robot hands in Isaac Sim with one camera on a desk stand instead of a VR headset. This post is the technical story behind it: the model, what it took to make it live, the performance work, an honest look at monocular depth, and the simulator side. All numbers are my own measurements on an RTX 3080 Ti laptop and, where noted, an RTX 5090.

TL;DR

  • The standard way to teleoperate robot hands in NVIDIA Isaac Lab is a hand-tracking headset (Apple Vision Pro or Meta Quest, streamed through CloudXR). I replaced it with one camera on a 3D-printed desk stand and NVIDIA’s EgoHand hand-pose model.
  • EgoHand ships as an offline video pipeline. Making it live meant a causal hand tracker, per-frame batching, online smoothing and a stream of OpenXR hand joints, the format headset retargeting already consumes.
  • Then the performance work: 120 → 58–70 ms per frame on a laptop GPU (fp16 with fp16 accumulation, CUDA graphs, a few surprisingly large inefficiencies), and tuning Isaac Sim’s renderer so that tracking and simulation run in real time together on one laptop, ~65 ms from camera to robot hand.
  • Monocular depth is the weak spot. Measured against a calibrated desk plane, EgoHand puts hands ~10–13 % too close: consistently at ≤ 0.9 m (correctable with one scale factor), and randomly beyond ~1 m (not correctable).
  • Everything is open source: github.com/timfmartin/egohand-live.

Why a camera instead of a headset

Hand-tracking headsets are the default input for dexterous teleoperation and for collecting robot demonstrations: NVIDIA’s Isaac Teleop stack streams the headset’s 26 OpenXR hand joints into Isaac Lab, where retargeters turn them into robot hand and arm commands. It works well, but it means a €330–€4,000 headset, wearing it for every session, and a CloudXR setup.

My question: how far does one ordinary camera get you? Two things made me optimistic:

  1. EgoHand is trained on egocentric video. A camera placed where your head would be, above and behind your hands looking down, sees hands the way the training data did. That is the whole idea behind the desk stand.
  2. The output can be the same as a headset’s. If the camera pipeline produces OpenXR-26 joints, everything downstream (retargeting, robot control, demo recording) stays NVIDIA’s standard path.
The desk stand (CAD model, with the camera's field of view in green) and what the camera sees: your hands from behind and above, as a head-mounted camera would.

The model: what EgoHand is

EgoHand comes from NVIDIA’s Isaac Capture project (the EgoPost pipeline). Per image it runs:

Stage What it does
RT-DETR hand detector (ONNX) boxes and left/right labels at 640 × 640
DINOv3 ViT-H+ backbone features of a 256 × 256 crop per hand
SOMA hand head pose and shape parameters of the SOMA hand model, a camera translation, the hand mesh

The camera model is perspective (sam3db_perspective): it gets the intrinsics, so with correct calibration the hand comes out in metric camera coordinates. Right hands go through the network as a mirrored image with a mirrored camera matrix (the model is trained on left hands).

EgoPost is built for whole clips: it tracks hands across the entire video in both directions, fills gaps, smooths bidirectionally and processes 32-frame batches. None of that works when the next frame does not exist yet.

Making it live

A causal tracker. Each frame, detections are matched to the previous frame’s tracks. Two lessons from live use:

  • Left/right from position, not from the detector. With palms facing the camera, the detector’s left/right class flips (a left palm looks like the back of a right hand). That produced mirrored, upside-down hand fits that stuck, because tracking kept the wrong label. With the camera behind the operator, the hand further left is the left hand, so the tracker assigns sides by image position and swaps tracks when hands cross.
  • A confidence filter against ghost hands. Desk clutter (a power socket, in my case) was detected as a hand with scores of 0.12–0.49. A track is now published only once a running average of its detector scores (EMA, α = 0.3) reaches 0.45 over three frames, and dropped below 0.25. Real hands survive brief low-score frames; clutter never gets published.

One frame, both hands, one batch. Both crops go through the network together.

A mirror shortcut. Instead of re-skinning the right hand’s mesh after the mirrored pass, I mirror the output geometrically: identical to the upstream path to < 0.6 mm, and ~20 ms faster on the 5090.

Online smoothing. A One Euro filter on wrist translation (with a separate, lower cutoff for depth, the noisiest axis) and on hand shape.

The output. SOMA’s 25 joints are converted to the 26 OpenXR hand joints, with orientations built from bone directions and the knuckle line (+Y out of the back of the hand, −Z toward the fingertips), and published as msgpack over ZeroMQ together with the camera pose. Anything can subscribe: the simulator, a viewer, a retargeting process.

More detail on the tracker, the output format and camera setup is in docs/tracking.md.

Performance: from 120 to ~60 ms per frame

On the RTX 5090 the first live version ran at ~22 FPS for two hands. On my RTX 3080 Ti laptop, the machine I wanted it to run on, together with Isaac Sim, it took ~120 ms per frame.

Where the time went

I profiled each stage with CUDA synchronisation, two hands:

The same numbers as a table
Stage Baseline After
Crop preparation 10.2 ms 2.1 ms
DINOv3 backbone 56.6 ms 26.4 ms
Pose head 12.7 ms 2.6 ms
SOMA (shape fit, skinning) 24.8 ms 10.4 ms
Rest of the forward pass 11.4 ms 7.1 ms

The backbone is compute-bound; the head and SOMA are launch-bound: many small kernels, each waiting for Python to issue it. That second category also explained a symptom I had seen: when other jobs loaded the laptop’s CPU, EgoHand slowed from ~95 ms to ~1 s per frame while the GPU sat idle.

What helped (cumulative, measured)

Change Per frame Notes
Baseline (bf16) 118–120 ms  
Store backbone weights in the compute dtype ~110 ms autocast re-cast ~840 M weights, ~5 GB of memory traffic, every frame; output identical
Stop padding a single hand to a batch of two   one-hand frames 85 → 77 ms predictor time
CUDA graphs, one per hand count 95 ms backbone, head and keypoint head replayed as graphs
fp16 with fp16 accumulation 86 ms see below
Precomputed SOMA regressor, GPU crops 72 ms SOMA’s shape fit 17.7 → 9.1 ms, 25 fewer host syncs
Detector through ONNX Runtime IO binding + CUDA graph 68 ms detector 12.4 → 10.2 ms
Per-block torch.compile inside the graphs 58–63 ms ~16 FPS

GPU memory dropped from 7.6 to 4.6 GB.

fp16 accumulation was the surprise. GeForce cards run fp16 matrix multiplies with fp32 accumulation at half the rate of fp16 accumulation (the data-centre parts don’t have this split). PyTorch defaults to fp32 accumulation; torch.backends.cuda.matmul.allow_fp16_accumulation switches it. fp16 with fp32 accumulation gave no gain over bf16; with fp16 accumulation the backbone got ~10 ms faster. And it was more accurate: against an fp32 reference, fp16 lands at 0.3–0.4 mm mean joint difference, bf16 at 1.3 mm (bf16 has fewer mantissa bits). Peak activations stayed ~24× below fp16’s limit, and a non-finite output would just drop that hand for a frame.

A CUDA graph gotcha. Capturing under torch.autocast with its default cast cache made the graph read freed memory on replay (an illegal memory access). Warm-up and capture need torch.autocast(..., cache_enabled=False). Also: graph outputs are overwritten by the next replay of the same shape, so anything that compares against another code path needs graphs off.

Robustness under CPU load is the less visible win. With 24 busy processes competing for the CPU, the worst 1 % of frames went from 725 ms to ~120 ms, because a replayed graph needs one launch instead of hundreds.

What didn’t help

  • Caching the hand-shape fit and refitting every N frames saved ~8 ms but moved joints by 3–7 mm mean (up to 29 mm): the per-frame shape estimate is entangled with the pose.
  • Fewer CPU threads (torch and OpenCV at one thread): no better under load.
  • ONNX Runtime NHWC layout: slower.
  • Graphing SOMA itself: not possible without modifying the upstream code (internal CPU-to-GPU copies, and warp kernels on their own stream).
  • Not tried yet: TensorRT (expected another 5–10 ms), and running the detector only when tracking is lost.

Calibration, and an honest look at monocular depth

Lens and camera pose

A webcam needs its lens measured once, and every placement needs the camera pose relative to the desk. Both come from a printed board:

  • Lens: a ChArUco board (layout and dictionary identified automatically from the first view) is detected even when partly outside the image, which matters for covering the corners where distortion is largest. Three lens models are fitted (OpenCV standard, rational, Kannala-Brandt fisheye) and the best kept. My cheap webcam: 0.14 px reprojection error, 57° field of view.
  • Camera pose: the board lying flat on the desk gives the desk plane by PnP, precise to about a millimetre, with no depth estimate involved. My first attempt used hands resting on the desk instead, which only works if the monocular depth is already good. It wasn’t.

Measuring the depth error

With the desk plane known, every hand resting flat on the desk has a known true distance along its viewing ray. That turns depth accuracy into something you can measure in a minute:

Setup Hand distance Depth error Correctable?
Webcam on the wall 1.2–1.3 m −15 to −24 %, ±12 % between spots no: random
Webcam on the desk stand ~0.9 m −13.3 % ± 3.1 % yes: scale 1.15
RealSense D405 on the stand 0.7–1.0 m −10.3 % ± 3.2 % yes: scale 1.11

Two takeaways:

  1. EgoHand puts hands consistently too close, by ~10–13 % in my setups. A single --depth-scale factor, applied by moving each hand along its viewing ray (so its position in the image does not change), fixes that to about ±3 %.
  2. Beyond about 1 m the error becomes random. At the wall-mounted 1.2 m, the error varied ±12 % from one resting spot to the next. Keep the camera within ~0.9 m of the hands, close to the head-to-hands distance the model was trained on.

A hypothesis that turned out wrong: I suspected the model’s estimate of hand size varied from pose to pose, which would translate directly into depth error. Recording a minute of tracking showed the estimated hand length varies by only ~4 %, against 12–15 % depth error. Size explains at most a third of it.

Into the simulator

Robot hands

Sharpa Wave hands (22 DoF each) in Isaac Lab 3, retargeted with dex-retargeting’s DexPilot using the same configuration and preprocessing as NVIDIA Isaac Teleop’s DexHandRetargeter. Fingers are retargeted in the wrist frame, which makes them largely insensitive to the depth error above.

The retargeting optimiser runs in a separate process. In a thread inside Kit it took ~300 ms per hand, starved by the GIL; as a child process, 5–15 ms. Later I also had to pin that process to one BLAS/OpenMP thread: with its default thread pool it fought Kit, PhysX and the tracker for cores and slowed to 100–200 ms per hand whenever the laptop got busy.

For arms, Franka’s official FR3 Duo asset gets a Sharpa hand on each flange, composed in USD into one articulation, and each tracked wrist drives its arm by damped least-squares IK.

For parallel grippers, the gripper follows the point between thumb and index fingertip, and a pinch closes it (below 3 cm; it opens above 5 cm, the rule of Isaac Teleop’s gripper retargeter). That drives Isaac Lab’s Franka cube-stacking task and records demonstrations in Isaac Lab’s HDF5 format.

Making Isaac Sim fast enough to share the GPU

With tracking on the same GPU, every millisecond of rendering is taken from EgoHand. What mattered, roughly in order:

  1. The renderer. Isaac Lab cannot choose Kit’s render mode, so Isaac Sim starts in RTX Real-Time 2.0 with DLSS, which NVIDIA’s own performance handbook notes can be slower on Ampere GPUs. Switching the viewport to RTX Minimal after start-up took the scene from 0.62× to 1.0× real time, and GPU load from 100 % to ~25 %. For videos I render in full quality afterwards (see below).
  2. sim.step() renders on every call unless told otherwise; Isaac Lab’s ManagerBasedRLEnv tasks render render_interval-often inside env.step (two to three renders per step in the stacking task).
  3. CPU PhysX instead of GPU PhysX for a scene with a few articulations: GPU physics pays a GPU-to-Python synchronisation for every state read.
  4. A pacing bug of my own. My wall-clock pacing reset its schedule whenever a step ran late. Render steps always run late, so the cheap physics steps after them waited a full step instead of catching up, capping the scene at ~0.75× real time.

And one trap: Isaac Lab 3 switched quaternions to XYZW. My gripper teleop assumed WXYZ and would have pointed the gripper upward.

End to end, on one laptop

  Measured
Tracking (one hand, webcam) 37–42 ms EgoHand, ~50 ms capture to result
Simulation (Sharpa hands, minimal rendering) 1.0× real time, ~6 ms per physics step
Retargeting 4–8 ms per hand (5–15 ms under heavier load)
Camera to robot hand moving ~65 ms

A footnote that cost me an evening: the laptop’s firmware occasionally clamps all CPU cores to 400 MHz (zero thermal-throttle events, normal temperatures, original charger), and everything (tracker, physics, retargeting) slows ~10× at once. grep MHz /proc/cpuinfo is now the first thing I check when the pipeline stutters.

Making the video

The video at the top is rendered offline from a recorded session, not screen-captured. (Recording sessions is in the public repository; the replay and capture tooling below isn’t released yet.)

  • run --save-session stores every frame the tracker saw with everything computed for it.
  • During recording the simulator runs in the light render mode, so tracking stays responsive.
  • Afterwards, the recorded hand stream is replayed into the simulator in full RTX quality (slower than real time, frames tagged with the original camera time), with depth and instance segmentation captured through Omniverse Replicator.
  • A renderer composes it: wireframe, the robot hand meshes (posed by the same DexPilot retargeting, drawn into the camera view from the hand’s URDF), the simulator view with the camera inset, and wipes into depth and segmentation.

Limitations

  • Monocular depth: calibrated to a few percent, but estimated, not measured. With a D405, fusing its depth sensor would give an absolute wrist distance.
  • A monitor instead of an immersive view, and no haptics.
  • The viewpoint matters: the camera has to look over your shoulders.

What’s next

A drop-in for the headset’s hand tracking

Isaac Teleop already lets device plugins replace the headset’s hands: NVIDIA’s Manus glove plugin pushes OpenXR hand joints into the CloudXR runtime through a “push device” extension, and every consumer downstream sees them instead of the headset’s. A camera plugin can do the same with all 26 joints, without any headset or XR client attached. Isaac Lab’s hand-driven tasks (GR1T2 pick-and-place, nut-pour, exhaust-pipe; Unitree G1 with Inspire hands; Galbot stacking) would then run unchanged on a monitor. Controller-driven tasks would not: the plugin replaces hand tracking only.

Status: to be confirmed with a spike. Push devices with no headset attached are not documented by NVIDIA.

Data collection for robot learning

The demo recording is there; LeRobot export and GR00T-Mimic augmentation are next.
